**Gross receipts are the total amount of money your business received from all sources during a set period, before subtracting any costs, expenses, refunds, or deductions.** In plain terms: add up every dollar that came in, and that total is your gross receipts.

It's a "top-line" number — the very first figure, before anything is taken out. The [IRS defines gross receipts](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/gross-receipts-defined) as "the total amounts the organization received from all sources during its annual accounting period, without subtracting any costs or expenses." That last part is the whole point: nothing gets subtracted.

## A plain-English definition

Break the phrase in two. *Gross* means "before deductions" — the whole amount, untrimmed. A *receipt*, in this sense, is money received; [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/receipt) defines a receipt as "something received." Put them together and gross receipts is literally "all the money received, before anything is taken out."

Three ideas are baked into the meaning:

- **All sources, not just sales.** Product sales, service fees, commissions, interest, rent you collect, even the sale of business equipment — if the money landed in your business, it counts.
- **Before any subtraction.** You do not deduct the cost of goods, business expenses, or the fees a payment processor took. Gross receipts is measured *before* all of that.
- **A period, not a moment.** Gross receipts is always tied to a time frame — a month, a quarter, or a tax year.

One more wrinkle: **bartered goods and services count too.** If you trade a $500 logo design for a $500 website, both of you record $500 of gross receipts at fair market value, even though no cash changed hands.

## What counts, and what doesn't

Here's the quick sorting rule. Almost everything coming *in* counts. The things that don't count are usually money you're only holding or handing straight back.

| Counts as gross receipts | Usually does *not* count |
|---|---|
| Sales of products | Refunds and returns you issue |
| Service and consulting fees | Sales tax you collect for the state (in many states) |
| Commissions and tips | Loan proceeds you have to repay |
| Interest and dividends earned | Money held in trust for someone else |
| Rent you collect | A customer's refunded deposit |
| Sale of business assets | — |

The trickiest one is **sales tax**. When you collect sales tax from a customer, that money isn't yours — you're holding it for the government. Because of that, many states let you exclude collected sales tax from gross receipts, while others include it unless it's specifically exempted. The rule varies by state, so check your own. (This is general information, not tax advice.)

## Gross receipts vs revenue vs gross sales

These three get used interchangeably, but they aren't identical.

- **Gross receipts** is the widest bucket: *every* dollar received, from *any* source.
- **Revenue** usually means money earned from your main business activity — your sales and services. It typically leaves out side items like interest or the one-time sale of an old laptop.
- **Gross sales** is narrower still: the total from selling your products or services, before discounts and returns.

An example makes the difference clear. Say a freelance designer earns $80,000 from client work, $200 in bank interest, and sells an old $300 iMac. Their **gross sales** are $80,000. Their **gross receipts** are $80,500 — every dollar received. In a simple service business with no side income, the numbers land in the same place, which is why people treat them as synonyms. The gap only shows up once money arrives from outside your core work.

Note what gross receipts is *not*: it's not profit, and it's not [accounts receivable](/blog/accounts-receivable-meaning). Profit is what's left after expenses. Accounts receivable is money you've invoiced but not yet collected — gross receipts counts money actually received.

## How to calculate gross receipts

The math is simple addition. The work is in gathering everything.

1. **Pick your period.** A month, a quarter, or the full tax year — decide the window first.
2. **Gather every record of money in.** Invoices you were paid on, sales receipts, 1099-NEC and 1099-K forms, cash and check payments, card deposits, interest statements — all of it.
3. **Add it all up.** Total every source of incoming money for the period.
4. **Don't subtract anything.** No expenses, no cost of goods, no processor fees. Gross receipts is the number *before* deductions.

**Worked example.** You run a small bakery. In one quarter you record:

```text
Cake and pastry sales      $42,000
Catering fees               $8,500
Sale of an old oven           $600
Interest on the business account   $50
----------------------------------------
Gross receipts             $51,150
```

Your gross receipts for the quarter are **$51,150**. Notice you don't subtract the $12,000 you spent on flour, rent, and wages — those are expenses that come off *later*, when you're figuring profit. And if a customer returned a $200 cake and you refunded them, you'd back that $200 out, because refunded money isn't a receipt you kept.

## Where gross receipts show up on your taxes

For most self-employed people, gross receipts isn't an abstract term — it's a specific line on a form. If you're a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, it goes on **Line 1 of [Schedule C (Form 1040)](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-schedule-c-form-1040)**, labeled "Gross receipts or sales."

The rule the IRS cares about: **report everything, not just what's on a 1099.** Line 1 includes every 1099-NEC and 1099-K you got, plus all the cash, checks, and card payments no one reported. If a client paid you $500 in cash and never sent a form, that $500 still belongs on Line 1. From there you subtract returns, cost of goods sold, and expenses further down the form to arrive at your net profit.

You don't need an LLC or a registered company for any of this — a sole proprietor invoicing under their own legal name reports gross receipts the same way. And you never put your Social Security number on the invoices themselves; that detail belongs on your tax return, not on documents you hand to clients.

## Don't confuse it with "gross receipts tax"

One quick clarification, because the search results mix them up. **Gross receipts** (the accounting term) is just a total. A **[gross receipts tax](https://taxfoundation.org/taxedu/glossary/gross-receipts-tax/)** is a specific state or local tax charged on that total, with no deduction for expenses.

A gross receipts tax is different from the two taxes you already know:

- **Unlike an income tax,** it doesn't let you deduct expenses first — it taxes your total intake, not your profit.
- **Unlike a sales tax,** it's charged to the *business*, not added to the customer's bill.

Only a handful of states levy one. According to the [Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/state-gross-receipts-taxes-2024/), states with a gross receipts tax include Delaware, Nevada, Ohio, Oregon, Tennessee, Texas (its franchise tax), and Washington. If you're not in one of those, you likely never deal with a gross receipts *tax* — but you'll still report gross receipts on your federal return.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Subtracting expenses too early.** Gross receipts is the *before* number. Costs come off later to get profit. Netting them out first understates your gross receipts.
- **Only counting 1099 income.** The IRS wants every dollar, including untracked cash and checks. Reporting just your 1099 totals leaves income off.
- **Forgetting non-sales income.** Interest, rent, and the sale of old equipment all count. It's not only your product sales.
- **Mishandling sales tax.** Collected sales tax is money you owe the state, not income you earned — and whether it belongs in gross receipts depends on your state's rule.
- **Confusing receipts with receivables.** Gross receipts is money actually received. An unpaid invoice sitting in [accounts receivable](/blog/accounts-receivable-meaning) isn't a receipt yet — it counts when the cash arrives. (General information, not tax advice — check with a professional for your situation.)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are gross receipts in simple terms?**

Gross receipts are the total amount of money your business took in from all sources during a period, before you subtract any costs, expenses, or refunds. If a dollar came into the business, it counts. It's the top-line "everything you received" number, measured before any deductions.

**Are gross receipts the same as revenue?**

Not exactly. Revenue usually means money earned from your core business activity, like sales and services. Gross receipts is broader — every dollar received from all sources, which can include interest, rent, or the sale of an asset. In many small service businesses the two are close or identical, but gross receipts is the wider bucket.

**Do gross receipts include sales tax?**

It depends on state law. Sales tax you collect from customers is money you're holding for the government, not income you earned, so many states let you exclude it. Others include it unless it's specifically exempted. Check your own state's rule, and treat this as general information, not tax advice.

**Where do gross receipts go on my tax return?**

If you're a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, they go on Line 1 of Schedule C (Form 1040). That line is your total business income from all sources — cash, checks, card payments, and every 1099 — before you subtract returns, cost of goods sold, or expenses.

**What is a gross receipts tax?**

A gross receipts tax is a state or local tax charged on a business's total receipts, with no deduction for expenses. It differs from an income tax (which taxes profit) and a sales tax (which is charged to the customer). Only a handful of states levy one, so most small businesses never touch it.

## In short

Gross receipts are every dollar your business received from all sources over a period, counted before you take out a single cost. Add up your sales, fees, and any other money in — don't subtract expenses — and that total is your gross receipts. It's the figure that lands on Line 1 of Schedule C, the starting point for the rest of your taxes, and a broader number than plain revenue or gross sales.
