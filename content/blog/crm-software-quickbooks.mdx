If you keep typing the same customer into your **CRM software** and then again into **QuickBooks**, an integration fixes that. Connect the two and a customer, invoice, or payment entered in one shows up in the other automatically. The best fits for most small businesses are **Method CRM** for the deepest QuickBooks sync, **HubSpot** for a free start, and **Insightly** or **Pipeline CRM** for seeing invoices and payments on the contact record. The rest of this guide covers how the sync actually works, which tool fits which business, and how to connect them without creating duplicates.

CRM stands for customer relationship management. [IBM defines a CRM](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/crm) as the technology a company uses to manage all its interactions with customers across sales, marketing, and service. QuickBooks, by contrast, is your accounting system—it holds invoices, payments, and the books. Integrating them means your sales side and your money side finally agree on who the customer is and what they owe. If you want the full primer on CRMs first, read our [plain-English guide to CRM software](/blog/crm-software).

## Does QuickBooks already have a CRM?

Sort of. QuickBooks Online now ships light CRM features through **Customer Hub**—lead tracking, automated follow-ups, referrals, and appointment scheduling, all inside the same app where your invoicing and payments already live. For a solo service business, that may be all the "CRM" you need, and it saves you from syncing anything.

But Customer Hub is not a full sales CRM. It has no deep pipeline stages, limited automation, and thin reporting compared to a dedicated tool. Once you have more leads than you can track in your head, multiple reps, or a real sales process with stages and forecasts, you will outgrow it. That is when people go looking for a separate CRM that *connects* to QuickBooks—so the sales team lives in the CRM while finance stays in QuickBooks, and the data flows between them.

One honest note before you shop: plenty of small operators do not need either. If your customer list is short and your real problem is billing clients and getting paid, skip the CRM entirely—our [free invoice generator](/invoice-generator) does that job on its own, and our [QuickBooks invoice template](/quickbooks-invoice-template) matches the look if you want it.

## The three ways to connect a CRM to QuickBooks

Every integration is one of these three, and knowing which you are buying saves a lot of grief.

**1. Native integration (built-in).** The CRM has an official QuickBooks connector you turn on from its marketplace. You log in with your Intuit account, authorize the link, and it starts syncing. This is the easiest and most reliable path—Intuit publishes a [help guide for connecting a CRM app to QuickBooks Online](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/learn-support/en-us/help-article/manage-integrations/connect-crm-app-get-sales-insights/L8UTkNV27_US_en_US) that walks through the authorization step. Choose this whenever it exists.

**2. Third-party connector.** When neither side has a native link, a connector like [Zapier's QuickBooks integrations](https://zapier.com/apps/quickbooks/integrations) or Make bridges them. You build automations: "when a deal is marked won in the CRM, create a customer in QuickBooks." Flexible, but each automation usually runs one direction, so a full two-way sync takes several of them and careful testing.

**3. Custom API build.** For unusual needs, a developer connects your CRM to the QuickBooks Online API directly. Most powerful, most expensive, and only worth it when off-the-shelf options genuinely cannot do what you need.

## One-way vs two-way sync (the part that matters most)

This single choice decides whether the integration helps or quietly creates chaos.

**One-way sync** copies data in a single direction. Typically QuickBooks pushes invoices and payments into the CRM as read-only, so reps can see a customer's balance without touching the books. Simpler, safer, and fine if the CRM only needs to *look at* financial data.

**Two-way sync** writes changes back both ways. Add a customer in the CRM and a matching customer appears in QuickBooks; mark an invoice paid in QuickBooks and the deal updates in the CRM. This is what most service businesses want, because nobody retypes anything—but it also means you must decide **who wins a conflict**. If a rep edits an address in the CRM while finance edits it in QuickBooks, one version has to overwrite the other, so check how your tool resolves that.

Two more things to confirm before you trust any sync:

- **Which records sync.** Customers and contacts, invoices, estimates, products, deals, payments—map exactly what moves. A connection that only syncs contacts won't give finance the revenue picture.
- **Real-time or batched.** Some tools sync instantly; others batch on a schedule. A few hours of lag means a rep might quote a customer while looking at a stale balance.

## How to sync a CRM with QuickBooks: step by step

The native path looks the same across most tools. Here is the full walkthrough.

1. **Pick your connection method.** Native if your CRM offers it (it almost certainly does for the tools above). Otherwise a connector like Zapier.
2. **Connect and authorize.** In the CRM, open the QuickBooks integration and sign in with your Intuit account. QuickBooks asks you to authorize the link and pick the company file. Approve it.
3. **Map your fields and sync direction.** Match CRM fields to QuickBooks fields—CRM "Company" to QuickBooks "Customer," CRM "Deal value" to an invoice, and so on. Choose one-way or two-way, and set which system wins a conflict.
4. **Run a small test sync.** Sync a handful of records first, not your whole database. Open both apps and confirm the customer, invoice, and payment landed correctly with no duplicates.
5. **Turn it on and monitor.** Switch on the full sync, then check it over the first week. Watch for duplicate customers and mismatched totals, and fix your field mapping if anything drifts.

### Worked example 1: a design studio on HubSpot

A five-person studio runs sales in HubSpot's free CRM and bills in QuickBooks Online. They install the QuickBooks integration from the HubSpot App Marketplace, authorize it with their Intuit login, and choose a **two-way sync** on customers and invoices. When a designer marks a deal "won," HubSpot creates the customer in QuickBooks and the studio generates the invoice there. Payment status flows back to the deal, so the salesperson sees "Paid" without opening QuickBooks. Result: the client's name is typed once, in HubSpot, and never again.

### Worked example 2: a plumbing company on Method

A plumbing outfit already lives in QuickBooks Desktop and wants a CRM that mirrors it exactly. They choose Method CRM for its deep native Desktop sync. Existing QuickBooks customers and open invoices flow into Method on the first sync. Office staff schedule jobs and log calls in Method; when they create an invoice there, it writes straight back to QuickBooks with no re-keying. Because it is two-way, they set **QuickBooks as the winner** on customer records, so the books stay the single source of truth for billing details.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Turning on a full two-way sync before testing.** A bad field map can create hundreds of duplicate customers in minutes. Always test on a few records first.
- **Not deciding who wins a conflict.** Skip this and the two systems fight over edits. Name QuickBooks the source of truth for financial fields.
- **Syncing only contacts.** If invoices and payments don't sync, finance still can't see revenue in the CRM and you've solved half the problem.
- **Duplicate customers from mismatched names.** "Acme Inc" in the CRM and "Acme, Inc." in QuickBooks become two records. Standardize names or use a unique ID to match.
- **Assuming Desktop and Online behave the same.** Many integrations support QuickBooks Online fully but Desktop only partially, or through an extra connector. Confirm your exact edition is covered.
- **Buying a CRM you don't need.** If billing is the real job, a CRM adds cost and clutter. Our [QuickBooks invoice guide](/blog/quickbooks-how-to-invoice) and [recurring invoice setup](/blog/quickbooks-recurring-invoice) may be all you need.
