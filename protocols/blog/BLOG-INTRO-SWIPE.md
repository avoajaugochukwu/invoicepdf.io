# Blog Intro Swipe File

## Opening paragraph patterns by intent

These are the patterns that win the first 8 seconds of attention on a blog post. The opening paragraph has two jobs in order: **answer the query in 40-60 words**, then **open a loop that earns the rest of the scroll**.

For definition and FAQ posts especially, the first body paragraph should double as the **40-60 word featured-snippet answer** — written so Google can lift it verbatim into a snippet. Lead with the answer, in a complete sentence, before you add depth.

The patterns below are real working shapes for invoicing posts. Use them as models, not templates.

---

## CRITICAL: Conversational, not copy

The fastest way to spot AI writing is in the opening paragraph. Choppy fragments, "Most people don't realize", "Here's the thing", "Let that sink in" — those are AI tells.

Your opening should sound like a person who has actually sent invoices and chased payments talking to a person who's stuck on one right now. Not a copywriter writing ad copy. Not an accounting textbook. A specific human who has spent time with the question. When you use a billing term for the first time — "Net 30", "purchase order", "remittance" — define it in the same breath. Your reader is a freelancer or small-business owner, not a finance expert.

The read-aloud test starts here. If the opening paragraph doesn't sound like something you'd say out loud, rewrite it.

---

## ANTI-AI SLOP: Opening contraband

| AI slop | Why it dies |
|---|---|
| "In today's fast-paced world..." | Generic. Earns no specificity. |
| "Have you ever wondered..." | Patronizing setup. Just ask the question. |
| "In this article, we will explore..." | Menu, not answer. Reader leaves. |
| "Most people don't know that..." | Condescending. Lazy. |
| "Whether you're a beginner or expert..." | Means nothing. Cut. |
| "Let's dive in." | Filler. |
| "Buckle up." | Filler. |
| "Here's what no one tells you..." | Pretentious. The reader can tell. |
| "What if I told you..." | Magician voice. |
| "Picture this:" | Cinematic preamble. Earn the scene. |
| "In the world of invoicing and billing..." | Throat-clearing. Start with the answer. |

## Pattern 1 — The Direct Answer Opener (informational queries)

**Use for:** definition, FAQ, cluster posts where the reader wants the answer first.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = the answer (and the snippet). Sentences 2-3 = the non-obvious depth. Reader gets the snippet payoff AND a reason to scroll.

### Examples

**"What does Net 30 mean" (definition cluster):**
> Net 30 is a payment term that means the client owes the full invoice amount within 30 days of the invoice date. It is the most common term freelancers and small businesses use, and it signals that you are extending the client a short, interest-free line of credit. The part that trips people up is when the clock starts: not the day the work finishes, but the date printed on the invoice.

**"How long does an invoice take to get paid" (FAQ):**
> On average, small-business invoices get paid in about 30 days, but the real range runs from same-day to well past 60, depending on the client and the terms you set. Larger companies tend to pay slower, often on their own internal cycle rather than yours. The surprise for most freelancers is how much of that timeline you control before you ever send the invoice, through the terms and the due date you choose.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Snippet bot loves the first sentence (and it is snippet-length)
- Reader gets value in 8 seconds
- "The part that trips people up" / "The surprise for most" opens the next loop

## Pattern 2 — The Cold Open (narrative posts, pillar essays, operator stories)

**Use for:** pillar essays, founder/operator stories, longform where the reader is here for the story.

**Shape:** Start mid-scene, in media res. Concrete, specific. The reader is dropped into a moment without context, and the post backfills as it goes.

### Examples

**Pillar essay opening (operator story about late payments):**
> Two years ago, my biggest client owed me $14,000 across four invoices, and not one of them was overdue. They were not late because I had never set a due date. I just attached a PDF, wrote "thanks!", and waited. The waiting stretched to eleven weeks before I admitted the problem was mine.

**News/update opening (a rule change that affects billing):**
> In April, a quiet change to the UK's late-payment rules raised the statutory interest a small business can charge on overdue invoices, and most freelancers never heard about it. The figure moved by a couple of percentage points. It does not sound like much. But for anyone billing five-figure projects on Net 30, that change is the difference between a polite nudge and a clause with teeth.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Specific, concrete, in-media-res hooks the reading reader
- The skimmer sees the H2 below it and decides whether to scroll back up
- "The waiting stretched to eleven weeks" / "the difference between" opens a stakes-bearing loop
- Trades snippet-eligibility for narrative pull — only use when narrative is the point

## Pattern 3 — The Stake-First Opener (commercial intent, comparison, decision posts)

**Use for:** comparison, decision posts, anywhere the reader is trying to decide between options.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = the stakes of the decision. Sentence 2-3 = who should pick A vs B in one breath.

### Examples

**Comparison opening (neutral competitor naming allowed here):**
> Choosing between a flat invoice tool and a full accounting suite matters more than it looks, because the wrong pick either leaves you paying for features you will never open or forces you to upgrade six months in. A free invoice generator is the sharper choice if you bill a handful of clients and just need a clean PDF that gets you paid. A suite like QuickBooks or Wave earns its keep only once you are tracking expenses, payroll, and taxes in one place. Most one-person businesses need the first long before they need the second.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Establishes stakes ("paying for features you will never open")
- Delivers the decision immediately
- "Most one-person businesses need the first long before they need the second" is an experience marker + complication that earns the rest of the post
- Competitors named neutrally — this is a genuine comparison post, not a place for a competitor CTA

## Pattern 4 — The Contrarian Opener (essays, hot-take posts)

**Use for:** posts that exist to push back on a common belief. High-friction, voice-forward.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = the common belief, stated fairly. Sentence 2 = why it's wrong (or incomplete). Sentence 3 = what the post will argue instead.

### Examples

**Contrarian opening:**
> The standard advice is to offer clients longer payment terms, like Net 60, to win bigger contracts. After three years of billing agencies that way, I have come to the opposite conclusion. Long terms do not win you the work; they just train good clients to pay you on the same slow schedule as their worst vendors, and they quietly hand you the cost of financing someone else's cash flow.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Doesn't strawman the common view
- "After three years of billing agencies that way" is an E-E-A-T experience marker
- "Financing someone else's cash flow" promises specific friction
- Reader either disagrees immediately (good — they read to argue) or agrees (also good)

## Pattern 5 — The Story-First Opener (case studies, customer-example posts)

**Use for:** case studies, posts built around a specific person or business situation.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = a specific moment. Sentence 2 = what was at stake. Sentence 3 = why this example matters.

### Examples

**Case-study opening:**
> In March, a freelance designer named Priya sent a $4,200 invoice to a marketing agency on Net 30 and assumed it would pay itself. Sixty-eight days later she was still waiting, and rent was due. She had done the work, delivered the files, and gotten a thumbs-up over email. The one thing she had not done was set a due date or a late fee, and that gap is exactly where the money got stuck.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Named person, specific amount, specific terms
- "Rent was due" is the stake
- The contrast (work delivered vs money stuck) is the hook for what the post will explore
- Reader has to scroll to learn what she changed

## Pattern 6 — The Specific Number Opener (data-driven, research-heavy posts)

**Use for:** posts built around statistics, survey findings, original data.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = the surprising number with full attribution. Sentence 2 = what it actually means. Sentence 3 = what the post will explain about it.

> **Note:** Any figure here goes through the accuracy-and-claims gate. Cite the survey, the year, and the jurisdiction, and link the primary source. A made-up percentage is a publish-blocker.

### Examples

**Data-driven opening:**
> A 2024 survey of UK small businesses found that 52% had invoices paid late in the previous year, with the average late payment worth several thousand pounds. The number matters less for the average than for the tail: a single client paying 90 days late can stall a one-person business entirely. This post is about the specific invoice terms that move that timeline, and the one clause most freelancers leave off.

**Why this pattern works:**
- The number is specific, sourced, and jurisdiction-stamped
- The interpretation ("matters less for the average than for the tail") goes beyond the bare stat
- Promise of one specific clause gives the reader a reason to scroll
- The "one clause most freelancers leave off" opens the next loop

## Pattern 7 — The Question Opener (FAQ, definition, explainer)

**Use for:** FAQ posts, definition cluster posts where the search query is itself a question.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = restate the question more precisely than the searcher phrased it. Sentence 2-3 = the short answer with the non-obvious nuance.

### Examples

**Question opening:**
> When people ask "do I need to charge VAT on my invoice," they are usually asking two different things, and the answer depends on which. If the question is whether you are legally required to add VAT — that hinges on whether you are VAT-registered, which in the UK is tied to a turnover threshold, not to how big any single invoice is. If the question is whether you should voluntarily register before you have to, that is a cash-flow judgment call, and it is the harder one.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Refines the search query (signals expertise)
- Two-track answer respects the actual complexity
- Defines VAT-registration as turnover-linked — jargon clarified on first use
- "That is the harder one" is a friction-bearing claim that earns the scroll

## Pattern 8 — The Practical Promise Opener (how-to, procedural)

**Use for:** how-to guides where the reader wants a specific result.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = the specific outcome the reader will be able to achieve. Sentence 2 = how long it takes. Sentence 3 = a credibility marker.

### Examples

**How-to opening:**
> By the end of this guide, you will have sent a clean, professional invoice that a client can pay without emailing you back for clarification — the right fields, a real due date, and your payment details all in one PDF. It takes about five minutes once you know what goes where. I have walked a dozen first-time freelancers through this exact sequence, and the same three fields are the ones everyone forgets.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Specific outcome (not "you'll invoice better")
- Time commitment quantified
- "A dozen first-time freelancers" + "the same three fields everyone forgets" = experience + a curiosity gap
- Reader knows whether to keep reading in 15 seconds

---

## Pattern 9 — The Friction Opener (wary readers, money-sensitive subjects)

**Use for:** posts where the reader is wary, stressed about money, or expects to be upsold.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = acknowledge the reader's likely state. Sentence 2 = state what this post will and won't do. Sentence 3 = the honest claim.

### Examples

**Friction opening:**
> If you searched "client won't pay invoice," you are probably stressed, a little angry, and tired of advice that ends in "just hire a lawyer." This post will not pretend a single email fixes every deadbeat client, and it will not tell you to eat the loss. What it will do is walk the actual escalation ladder — the reminder, the late-fee notice, the formal demand — in the order that gets most invoices paid before anyone needs a lawyer at all.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Names the reader's likely state honestly
- Sets expectations the post can deliver on (no overpromising)
- "Before anyone needs a lawyer at all" is the friction-payoff promise
- High trust signal in 60 words

## Pattern 10 — The Cross-Reference Opener (cluster posts in a hub)

**Use for:** cluster posts that fit inside a larger pillar.

**Shape:** Sentence 1 = the specific question this post answers. Sentence 2 = how it relates to the broader topic. Sentence 3 = where to go next or what to read first.

### Examples

**Cross-reference opening:**
> The difference between an invoice and a receipt trips up almost every new freelancer, and getting it wrong can mean a client pays late or your books do not reconcile. The short version: an invoice is a request for payment you send before money changes hands, and a receipt is proof of payment you send after. This post unpacks the distinction with examples; [the full guide to invoicing basics](/blog/invoicing-basics) covers the whole document set you will need.

**Why this pattern works:**
- Names the specific question precisely
- Drops the answer in sentence 2
- Internal link to the pillar in sentence 3 establishes topical authority
- Reader has the answer AND a path to deeper context

## The four opening mistakes the audit catches

### Mistake 1 — Burying the answer
> "Today, we'll explore the question of what Net 30 really means and why it matters for modern freelancers. To understand this, we first need to look at the history of trade credit..."

**Fix:** lead with the answer. The reader can leave after one paragraph if you've satisfied them — that's a win, not a loss.

### Mistake 2 — Generic abstractions
> "Invoicing is one of the most important parts of running a business. Throughout history, people have needed to bill each other for goods and services..."

**Fix:** specifics. A specific term, a specific amount, a specific client situation, a specific number. Abstractions are the AI default.

### Mistake 3 — Empty hype
> "What I'm about to share will completely transform how you get paid. Most freelancers have no idea about this one powerful invoicing trick..."

**Fix:** the post either delivers the result or it doesn't. Stating it doesn't make it so. Lead with the substance.

### Mistake 4 — The lecture preamble
> "Before we begin, it's important to define our terms. There are many different types of invoices and payment terms to consider..."

**Fix:** if the terms need definition, define them inline as you use them. The preamble is throat-clearing.

## What every opening must contain

Regardless of pattern, every opening paragraph contains:

1. **The target query addressed** (often verbatim, often in a sharpened restatement)
2. **A specific element** (a number, a term, a named example, an amount) that an AI couldn't have generic'd
3. **A signal of who the author is** (first-person, experience marker, or a cited source)
4. **An open loop** (question, complication, or promise that earns the next paragraph)

If any of those four is missing, the opening is weaker than it could be.
